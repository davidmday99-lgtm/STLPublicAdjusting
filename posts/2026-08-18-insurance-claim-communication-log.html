Claim organization

Build a claim communication log that actually helps.

August 18, 20266 minute readSimple claim system

A property claim can generate dozens of calls, emails, photographs, estimates, invoices, payment letters, and document requests. The problem is not just volume. Important details get scattered across phones and inboxes, making it difficult to answer basic questions: What did the adjuster request? When did we send it? Which issue is still open?

A claim communication log turns that pile into a timeline. It does not need special software. A notebook, spreadsheet, or shared document can work if you use it consistently.

The goal: Someone unfamiliar with the claim should be able to read the log and understand what happened, what was exchanged, what is still disputed, and what should happen next.

The six fields every entry needs

  1. Date and time. Record when the communication happened—not when you remembered to enter it.
  2. People involved. Include names, roles, company, phone number, and email when available.
  3. Communication type. Call, voicemail, email, portal upload, letter, inspection, payment, or in-person meeting.
  4. Short factual summary. Write what was asked, stated, sent, or decided without editorial comments.
  5. Documents. List the exact file names or attachments connected to the entry.
  6. Next action. Identify who is doing what and the expected follow-up date.

A useful entry in practice

August 18, 2026, 10:15 a.m. — phone call with Jordan Smith, desk adjuster. Jordan asked for the mitigation invoice and photographs of the opened kitchen wall before reviewing added drywall and insulation. I will email files “Mitigation-Invoice-1042.pdf” and “Kitchen-Wall-01.jpg” through “Kitchen-Wall-06.jpg” today. Jordan said to expect confirmation within two business days. Follow up August 21 if no receipt is confirmed.

That entry is much more useful than “Called insurance. Sent pics.”

Build five simple claim folders

Keep the log beside an organized document library. Use file names that make sense outside your inbox.

  • 01 Policy: declarations, policy form, endorsements, renewal documents
  • 02 Carrier: letters, estimates, payment explanations, emails, portal confirmations
  • 03 Damage: photographs and video grouped by room, elevation, or component
  • 04 Costs: contractor estimates, invoices, receipts, temporary repairs, additional living expenses
  • 05 Reports: mitigation logs, engineering, inspections, material information, inventories

Consider adding the date to every file name in year-month-day format: “2026-08-18-carrier-estimate.pdf.” The files will sort themselves chronologically.

Track documents as sent and received

For every submission, note the delivery method and confirmation. Email may have a sent timestamp. A claim portal may generate a confirmation number or screenshot. Certified mail may have tracking. The point is not to create paperwork for its own sake; it is to avoid redoing work or debating whether a document arrived.

Keep original photographs and documents unchanged. If you annotate a photo, save the marked version as a copy. If a revised estimate replaces an earlier one, keep both and label the versions.

Separate facts, questions, and disagreements

A useful claim record distinguishes three things:

  • Fact: “The carrier estimate includes 120 square feet of drywall in the family room.”
  • Question: “Does that quantity include the adjacent wet wall shown in photographs 12–16?”
  • Position: “The contractor estimates 205 square feet based on field measurements.”

Keeping those categories clear leads to better written questions and more productive responses.

Use an open-items list

Your communication log is chronological; your open-items list is a dashboard. Review it weekly and include:

  • Requested document not yet provided
  • Submitted item awaiting confirmation or decision
  • Inspection or estimate not yet scheduled
  • Coverage question needing a written answer
  • Estimate difference requiring explanation
  • Payment received but not matched to an estimate or letter

What not to put in the log

Avoid passwords, Social Security numbers, full banking information, or unrelated personal details. Store sensitive documents securely and share them only through appropriate channels. The claim log should be useful without becoming a new privacy risk.

If communication stalls

Send a concise follow-up that references the prior request, the date it was sent, and the specific response needed. Missouri’s insurance department advises consumers to document calls and keep copies of written communication when a dispute arises. If normal follow-up does not resolve the concern, the Missouri and Illinois insurance departments publish consumer assistance and complaint information.
