Claim supplements

What is an insurance claim supplement—and when should you ask for one?

August 18, 20268 minute readSt. Louis, Missouri + Illinois

An insurance estimate is a starting point based on the information available at the time it was written. Once a contractor opens a wall, matches a discontinued material, measures the full repair area, or confirms a code-related requirement, the real scope can look different. A supplemental claim is the process of asking the insurer to consider additional documented costs that were not fully addressed in the original estimate or payment.

Plain-language definition: A supplement is a documented request for the insurer to review added or revised claim costs. It is not an automatic payment, and it does not change the policy. The carrier still evaluates whether each requested item is covered and supported.

Why supplements happen

Property repairs are rarely as tidy as a first inspection suggests. The Missouri Department of Commerce and Insurance describes supplements as additional payments beyond the initial settlement that may help address unexpected costs not fully included in the original estimate. Common reasons include:

  • Hidden damage becomes visible during reasonable demolition.
  • The original estimate omitted part of the affected area.
  • Material availability or matching changes the repair approach.
  • A contractor documents different quantities, labor steps, or access needs.
  • A building official identifies a requirement that affects covered repairs.
  • Temporary protection, drying, testing, or other necessary services were not included.

None of those circumstances guarantees coverage. The cause of loss, policy wording, exclusions, endorsements, deductible, limits, and prior damage can all affect the decision.

Start with a line-by-line comparison

“The contractor’s number is higher” is not enough information for a useful review. Put the carrier estimate and contractor estimate next to each other. Identify the exact differences:

  • Which room, elevation, roof slope, or building component is involved?
  • Is the item missing, measured differently, or priced differently?
  • What photograph, invoice, measurement, report, or code reference supports it?
  • Was the condition visible during the first inspection?
  • Has work started, and if so, what new condition was discovered?

A clean comparison lets the adjuster respond to specific items instead of treating the request as a general disagreement.

What belongs in a supplement package

1. A short cover note

State the claim number, property address, date of loss, and purpose of the submission. Briefly explain what changed since the prior estimate. Keep opinions separate from facts.

2. The revised estimate

Use a detailed estimate with room names, measurements, quantities, materials, labor operations, and unit prices. A single lump-sum bid makes it harder to compare scopes.

3. Labeled photographs

Show the location and condition in context, then add close-ups. Label files with the room or component and date. If demolition exposed the condition, include before, during, and after photographs when available.

4. Supporting documents

Depending on the item, this may include invoices, drying logs, material availability information, measurements, engineer or specialist reports, permit information, and written communication from the contractor.

5. A request for a written response

Ask the carrier to identify which items are accepted, which are not, and the policy or factual basis for any disagreement. A written response keeps the file understandable.

When should you submit it?

Report material changes promptly and follow the communication and documentation requirements in your policy. If hidden damage is found during work, photograph it before the area is closed. Contact the carrier or adjuster before incurring major additional costs when practical, and ask what inspection or documents they need.

Do not wait until the end of the project to reveal months of added work if the insurer could reasonably have inspected it earlier. At the same time, do not rush an incomplete package just to say it was submitted. A timely, organized request is easier to evaluate than a stream of disconnected texts and invoices.

Questions to ask after submission

  • Did you receive the complete supplement package?
  • Is another inspection needed?
  • Who is responsible for reviewing the request?
  • Is any specific document missing?
  • Which line items remain disputed, and why?
  • Will an approved amount be paid now or after repair documentation is provided?

If the supplement is only partly approved

Separate the agreed and disputed items. Confirm that approved additions appear in a revised estimate, then focus on the remaining differences. Check whether the disagreement is about coverage, cause of damage, repair method, quantity, price, or documentation—each problem calls for a different response.

If you cannot resolve the issue with the company, Missouri and Illinois insurance departments provide consumer assistance and complaint resources. A public adjuster may also help with a complex loss, but consumers should understand the services, contract, fees, and licensing before hiring one.
