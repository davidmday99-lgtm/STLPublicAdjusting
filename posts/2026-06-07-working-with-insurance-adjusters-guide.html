Working With Insurance Adjusters: Complete Guide for St. Louis Property Owners

June 7, 2026

The insurance adjuster assigned to your claim is a critical figure in your property damage settlement process. Understanding who they represent, what their role involves, and how to work effectively with them directly impacts the outcome of your claim.

Many property owners treat adjusters with uncertainty—mixing respect for their expertise with concern about whether they're truly advocates for their interests. In reality, adjusters are employees of the insurance company, not neutral parties. This distinction shapes every interaction.

This comprehensive guide explains the adjuster relationship, clarifies their role and authority, and provides strategies for productive collaboration with insurance adjusters on your Missouri or Illinois property damage claim.

Understanding Your Insurance Adjuster

Who Is the Adjuster?

Your insurance adjuster is a licensed professional employed by or contracted to your insurance company. They are assigned to your claim to:

  • Verify coverage exists for the claimed loss
  • Investigate the circumstances of the damage
  • Determine the scope of covered damage
  • Prepare or review the repair estimate
  • Make recommendations for claim payment

Critical point: Your adjuster works for the insurance company, not for you. Their job is to accurately assess the loss within the policy terms—which often means finding reasons to minimize payment when possible.

Carrier Adjuster vs. Independent Adjuster

Carrier Adjuster (Staff Adjuster):

  • Employed directly by your insurance company
  • Handle claims year-round for that carrier
  • More familiar with specific carrier policies and procedures
  • May handle higher volume of claims
  • More likely to apply consistent company standards

Independent Adjuster (IA):

  • Hired by the insurance company for this specific claim or disaster
  • Often assigned after major storms when carrier adjusters are overwhelmed
  • Licensed professionals with diverse experience
  • May have less familiarity with specific carrier quirks
  • Still represent the insurance company's interests, not yours

Catastrophe (CAT) Adjusters

After major storms in Missouri and Illinois, catastrophe adjusters are deployed:

  • Brought in specifically for disaster response
  • Often from outside the region
  • May not be familiar with local building codes or costs
  • Under pressure to process high volume quickly
  • Sometimes less detailed in initial inspections

The Adjuster Inspection Process

Pre-Inspection Preparation

Before the adjuster visits your property, prepare thoroughly:

  • Gather documentation: Insurance policy, photos taken immediately after event, receipts for damage mitigation
  • Prepare the property: Make safe pathway for adjuster access; clear debris to reveal damage
  • Organize records: Keep home inventory, purchase receipts, warranty documentation nearby
  • Create damage list: Compile list of damage items noticed, organized by location
  • Arrange access: Ensure adjuster can inspect all areas—attic, basement, crawlspace
  • Safety first: Address immediate hazards (loose electrical wiring, water accumulation)

During the Inspection

What to Expect:

  • Adjuster will conduct physical inspection of damaged areas
  • They'll take photos and measurements
  • They'll ask questions about the loss and timeline
  • They'll look for any signs of pre-existing damage or maintenance issues
  • They may access attic, basement, and other hidden areas
  • Inspection typically takes 1-4 hours depending on claim complexity

What You Should Do:

  • Stay present: Accompany the adjuster throughout inspection
  • Take notes: Document what adjuster photographs and areas covered
  • Point out damage: Direct adjuster to all problem areas; don't assume they'll find everything
  • Provide documentation: Share photos, contractor estimates, and documentation prepared beforehand
  • Ask clarifying questions: Request explanation of anything you don't understand
  • Record names: Get adjuster's full name, title, contact information, badge number
  • Ask about timeline: When will estimate be provided? Who will you contact with questions?
Documentation During Inspection: Take photos of the adjuster inspecting damage areas, taking measurements, photographing damage. This creates independent record of what was examined.

What Adjusters Miss

Adjusters don't always catch everything, especially on first inspection:

  • Secondary water damage: Interior damage from roof leaks may develop gradually
  • Hidden damage: In walls, attic spaces, behind appliances
  • Specialty items: HVAC contamination, ductwork damage requiring system-wide replacement
  • Code upgrade requirements: Missouri building code items not yet identified
  • Future mold growth: Early-stage moisture conditions that will develop into mold
  • Settling and structural problems: Secondary structural failures from water damage

This is why supplemental claims are common—and often necessary.

Communication Strategy With Adjusters

Setting Expectations

  • Designate single contact: Assign one person as claim contact to prevent confusion
  • Request contact method preferences: Email preferred over phone for documentation
  • Establish response timeline: Request specific date for estimate delivery
  • Ask about escalation process: Who is adjuster's supervisor if issues arise?
  • Request claim number: Get claim number for all future communications

Email Communication Best Practices

  • Follow up phone calls with email summary: "Per our conversation on [date], you indicated..."
  • Include specific claim details: Claim number, property address, date of loss
  • Be professional but firm: Polite but clear about your expectations
  • Document everything: Keep copies of all correspondence
  • Ask for written confirmation: "Please confirm via email that you received my documentation"
  • Track deadlines: Reference Missouri response requirements in communications

Managing Disagreements

If you disagree with the adjuster's assessment:

  • Ask for explanation: "Why was that item excluded from the estimate?"
  • Provide supporting documentation: Contractor estimates showing why item should be included
  • Don't be confrontational: Maintain professional relationship while advocating for position
  • Request supervisor review: If adjuster won't budge, ask for supervisor consultation
  • Know when to escalate: Consider professional representation for significant disputes

Adjuster Authority and Limitations

What Adjusters Can Do

  • Investigate the claim and verify coverage
  • Prepare or review repair estimates
  • Approve claim payment up to their authority limit (often $10,000-$50,000)
  • Request additional documentation or photos
  • Recommend supplemental payments for additional discovered damage
  • Explain policy coverage to you

What Adjusters Cannot Do

  • Deny coverage: Claim denials typically require claims manager or legal review
  • Exceed their authority: Large claims require management approval
  • Waive policy limits or exclusions: Only insurance company legal can modify policy terms
  • Guarantee final settlement amount: Adjusters make recommendations, not final decisions
  • Bind the insurance company: Verbal agreements don't change policy terms

Missouri Tip: If adjuster won't approve something within their authority, request escalation to their supervisor or management. Document request in writing.

Red Flags: When to Get Professional Help

Consider hiring a public adjuster when:

  • Adjuster unresponsive: No contact for 14+ days, missed promised deadlines
  • Estimate seems low: Multiple contractor quotes exceed adjuster estimate by 20%+
  • Obvious items missing: Damage clearly visible but not on adjuster's estimate
  • Pre-existing condition claim: Adjuster blaming previous damage for storm damage
  • Policy provision conflicts: Adjuster interpreting expansive exclusions or limitations
  • Pressure to settle quickly: Adjuster rushing you to accept inadequate offer
  • Communication breaking down: Can't get straight answers to reasonable questions
  • Large claim complexity: Claims over $20,000 warrant professional review

Understanding Adjuster Estimates

Estimate Components

When you receive the adjuster's estimate, review:

  • Scope of work: Detailed description of repairs to be performed
  • Square footage/quantities: Specific measurements for each item
  • Unit pricing: Cost per square foot, per item, or per unit of measure
  • Labor costs: Separate from material costs; Missouri-specific rates
  • Tax treatment: Missouri sales tax (4.225% state + local rates)
  • Deductible: Amount withdrawn from overall settlement
  • Depreciation holdback: If replacement cost policy, percentage or dollar amount held pending completion

Comparing Estimates

Always get independent contractor estimates to compare with adjuster's estimate:

  • Same format: Request same line-item breakdown for comparison
  • Multiple quotes: Get 2-3 contractor perspectives on scope and pricing
  • Local expertise: Ensure contractors familiar with St. Louis or region-specific costs
  • Document differences: Note specific line items where contractor quote exceeds adjuster estimate
  • Justify replacements: Show why item should be replaced vs. repaired

Supplemental Claim Process

Additional damage often emerges after initial inspection:

When Supplementals Are Appropriate

  • Water damage that develops gradually over days/weeks post-storm
  • Hidden damage discovered during repair process
  • Secondary damage from initial covered damage (e.g., mold from water intrusion)
  • Structural problems that emerge as repairs begin
  • Code upgrade requirements identified during repair planning

Filing Supplementals

  • Document promptly: Photos and descriptions when discovered
  • Provide contractor assessment: Professional evaluation of new damage
  • Tie to original loss: Clearly explain how new damage relates to storm/covered event
  • Submit in writing: Formal supplemental request with supporting documentation
  • Provide estimate: Specific cost for repairs, line items for comparison

Working With CAT Adjusters After Major Storms

Catastrophe adjusters after major Missouri storms require special strategies:

Challenges With CAT Adjusters

  • Unfamiliar with local St. Louis/regional building standards
  • Working under extreme time pressure (hundreds of claims pending)
  • May not be available for follow-up questions
  • Less likely to catch subtle secondary damage
  • Often rely heavily on initial photos provided

Strategies for CAT Claims

  • Provide thorough pre-inspection documentation: Your own photos critical when adjuster can't be thorough
  • Request local adjuster transfer: Ask if claim can be reassigned to local staff adjuster after initial CAT phase
  • Document CAT adjuster limitations: Note areas CAT adjuster didn't thoroughly inspect for later supplementals
  • Get contact information: Name, badge number, company for follow-up questions
  • Plan for supplementals: Understand that CAT estimates often underestimate secondary damage

Your Rights With Insurance Adjusters

Missouri law protects your rights in claim investigations:

  • Right to representation: You can have attorney or public adjuster present during inspection
  • Right to documents: Request copies of adjuster's report, photos, estimates
  • Right to reasonable investigation: Insurer must conduct fair, complete investigation
  • Right to prompt handling: Missouri law requires timely claim response and payment
  • Right to appeal: Disagree with adjuster findings; request supervisor review

Key Takeaways

  • Adjusters work for insurance company: Not neutral; representing carrier interests
  • Prepare thoroughly for inspection: Documentation and property preparation maximize adjuster's scope review
  • Stay involved: Accompany inspector, point out damage, take notes
  • Communicate in writing: Email creates documentation trail for all discussions
  • Get professional comparison: Contractor estimates essential for estimate verification
  • Know adjuster authority: Understand what they can approve vs. when escalation needed
  • Plan for supplementals: Initial inspection won't catch all damage; be proactive about follow-ups
  • Escalate when appropriate: Professional representation justified for significant disputes

Effective adjuster relationships are based on professionalism, clear communication, and mutual respect—while remembering that you have advocates available when needed. By understanding the adjuster's role and employing these strategies, you can work effectively toward a fair settlement.
