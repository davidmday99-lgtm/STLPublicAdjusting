Your Duty to Mitigate: What Missouri Insurance Policies Require After Property Damage

March 27, 2026

When disaster strikes your St. Louis home—whether from a spring thunderstorm, winter pipe burst, or kitchen fire—your insurance policy doesn't just cover the damage. It also imposes important responsibilities on you as the homeowner. One of the most critical (and often overlooked) duties is your obligation to mitigate further damage after a loss occurs.

Missouri insurance policies contain specific language requiring policyholders to take reasonable steps to prevent additional damage. Fail to do this, and you could find your claim reduced or even denied—even for damage that would otherwise be covered.

Understanding what mitigation means, and what actions are reasonable, can make the difference between a smooth claims process and a frustrating battle with your insurance company.

What Is the Duty to Mitigate?

The duty to mitigate (sometimes called "damage control" or "loss prevention") is a standard provision in virtually all property insurance policies in Missouri. It requires you to:

  • Take reasonable steps to protect your property from further damage after a loss occurs
  • Prevent avoidable additional losses
  • Act promptly—not wait days or weeks to address ongoing issues

This duty exists because insurance is designed to cover sudden, accidental losses—not damage that results from neglect or inaction after the initial event. If a pipe bursts on Tuesday and you don't shut off the water or call a plumber until Friday, the insurer may argue that the worsened damage from those extra days is your responsibility, not theirs.

Why Insurance Companies Enforce This Duty

From the insurer's perspective, the duty to mitigate serves several purposes:

  • Prevents avoidable losses: It encourages homeowners to act quickly to stop damage from spreading (like tarping a roof to prevent rain intrusion)
  • Controls claim costs: By limiting the extent of damage, mitigation helps keep claims—and premiums—more affordable for everyone
  • Determines causation: It helps distinguish between damage caused by the covered event and damage caused by the homeowner's failure to act
  • Promotes responsibility: Insurance is a partnership—policyholders have obligations just as insurers do

In practice, adjusters and claims examiners in Missouri routinely review what actions you took (or didn't take) after discovering damage. Your mitigation efforts—or lack thereof—can significantly impact your settlement.

Common Examples of Reasonable Mitigation

What constitutes "reasonable" mitigation depends on the situation, but here are typical examples adjusters expect to see in the St. Louis area:

Water Damage:

  • Shutting off the water supply at the main valve
  • Calling a plumber or water mitigation company
  • Using towels, mops, or wet/dry vacuums to remove standing water
  • Moving furniture and belongings away from wet areas
  • Opening windows and using fans to promote drying (when weather permits)
  • Tarping a roof leak to prevent further water intrusion

Fire Damage:

  • Contacting the fire department to ensure the fire is completely out
  • Covering openings in the roof or walls to prevent weather damage
  • Turning off utilities if they pose a safety hazard
  • Securing the property to prevent vandalism or theft

    • Storm/Wind Damage:

    • Covering broken windows or roof holes with tarps or plywood
    • Removing fallen trees or branches that pose additional risks
    • Securing loose siding, gutters, or shutters
    • Documenting damage before making temporary repairs

    Theft/Vandalism:

    • Filing a police report promptly
    • Securing the property (boarding up windows, changing locks)
    • Making temporary repairs to prevent weather damage

    The key is that these actions should be reasonable and temporary—you're not expected to make permanent repairs, just to prevent the situation from getting worse while you work through the claims process.

    What Happens If You Fail to Mitigate?

    Insurance companies in Missouri can deny or reduce coverage for damage that results from your failure to mitigate. Here's how it typically works:

    Example 1: Ongoing Water Leak
    A pipe bursts under your kitchen sink on Monday morning. You notice it but decide to "wait until the weekend" to call a plumber. By Saturday, the water has ruined your cabinets, warped your hardwood floors, and seeped into the basement. The adjuster determines that while the initial pipe burst is covered, the damage to the floors and basement resulted from your failure to mitigate and denies coverage for those portions.

    Example 2: Uncovered Roof After Storm
    A severe thunderstorm tears off a section of your roof in Chesterfield on Tuesday. You see the hole but don't tarp it, assuming "the insurance will handle it." Over the next five days, two more rainstorms soak your attic, damaging insulation, ceiling drywall, and personal belongings. The insurer may cover the original storm damage to the roof but deny the interior water damage as preventable.

    Example 3: Broken Window After Break-In
    Your St. Louis city home is burglarized on a Friday night, with a window smashed to gain entry. You leave the broken window unrepaired over the weekend, and another storm drives rain into your living room, damaging electronics and furniture. The theft-related damage (broken window, stolen items) is likely covered, but the rain damage from the unrepaired opening may be denied.

    In each case, the adjuster applies what's called the "efficient proximate cause" doctrine—determining whether the damage was directly caused by the covered peril or by your inaction afterward.

    Practical Steps for St. Louis Homeowners

    How can you fulfill your duty to mitigate without overstepping or interfering with the claims process? Follow these guidelines:

    1. Act quickly, but safely: Address immediate hazards (electrical, structural) first, then take reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Never put yourself at risk.
    2. Document everything: Before you do anything, take photos and videos of the damage. Continue documenting as you take mitigation steps—this shows you were acting responsibly.
    3. Keep receipts: Save all receipts for materials, supplies, or services related to mitigation (tarps, plywood, plumber visits, etc.). These are typically reimbursable as part of your claim.
    4. Make temporary repairs only: Your goal is to stabilize the situation, not to remodel. Leave permanent repairs for after the claim is settled.
    5. Notify your insurer promptly: Call your insurance company's claims line the same day or next business day to report the loss and get a claim number.
    6. Consider professional help: For significant water damage, mold risk, or structural issues, contacting a mitigation professional is often the wisest choice—and shows you're taking your duty seriously.

    When Mitigation Gets Complicated

    Sometimes determining what's "reasonable" isn't straightforward. Consider these St. Louis-specific scenarios:

    Historic Homes: If you own a historic property in Lafayette Square or Soulard, using inappropriate materials for temporary repairs (like modern plywood on ornate woodwork) could cause additional concerns. Consult with professionals familiar with historic preservation.

    Condos and Townhomes: In multi-family buildings common throughout the metro area, your mitigation efforts might affect neighboring units. Coordinate with your HOA or property manager when possible.

    Sewer Backups: After heavy rains overwhelm St. Louis' combined sewer system, basement backups are common. Mitigation here requires specialized cleanup due to health risks—don't attempt sewage cleanup yourself.

    Extended Absences: If you're a snowbird who spends winters in Florida, arrange for a neighbor, friend, or property management company to check your St. Louis home regularly and act quickly if damage occurs.
